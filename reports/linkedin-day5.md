Every community pharmacist I know has been saying the same thing for a decade: you don't set the price you're paid, and you don't set the price you pay.

Twenty years of USDA SNAP retailer data shows what that costs — through a comparison I did not expect to find.

Structurally, a chain pharmacy and a fuel-forward convenience chain are the same animal. The Wawas, Casey's and Sheetzes of the world are small chain stores with a second business running behind the counter. So is your pharmacy. The front of the store sells snacks and sundries; the economics live in back.

In the early 2020s that second business got dramatically better for the gas stations. Murphy USA's margin on a gallon of fuel rose 103%. They rode it up — those chains grew 9.1% between 2021 and 2025, and dollar stores grew 8.5%.

At that exact same moment, chain pharmacies fell 24.3%. Down from a peak of 20,341 SNAP-authorized locations in 2016, with 5,054 authorizations ending in four years.

Same country. Same four years. Same store shape. Opposite direction.

Nothing about the store explains that. Everything about the margin does. The gas station's core business had expanding margins. The pharmacy's had contracting ones — and a pharmacy sets neither end of its own: the PBM decides what the script pays, and the primary wholesaler agreement decides most of what the drug costs.

One format thrived. The other is in demise.

---

Two things I left out of the post.

First, this is also a food story. USDA files a drug store in the same category as a dollar store — "Combination Grocery/Other" — and in a lot of communities that store is where people buy food. 976 ZIP codes that had a SNAP-authorized chain pharmacy in 2021 had none by 2025. One in six nonmetro ZIP codes lost its last one, against roughly one in nine metro ones. A town loses a place to buy food and its most reachable health professional on the same day.

Second, and I want to be straight about it: this data cannot see independent pharmacy. A store only appears in it if it's SNAP-authorized, which requires stocking staple foods, and most independents aren't. Against roughly 19,000 independents nationally, 307 turn up here. So everything above is a chain story — but it's the story community pharmacy has been living for years. The chains just got there last.
